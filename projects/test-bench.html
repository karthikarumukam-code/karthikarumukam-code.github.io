Predicting Test-Bench Time

A model that estimates how long a car configuration will need on the test bench before the car arrives, built from 4,209 recorded runs of the Mercedes-Benz Greener Manufacturing dataset.

The problem

The test bench is a shared, limited resource, and every finished configuration has to pass through it before production. Most cars clear it quickly. Some take far longer, and today nobody knows which until the car is already on the bench.

The question: can the time on the bench be predicted from the configuration, before the car arrives?

The same data is a public Kaggle competition. On the competition's private-leaderboard metric the model scored 0.543, against 0.556 for the documented first-place solution. Our first reading of that benchmark was wrong; we caught it, verified the real documented score and corrected the claim before presenting.

What actually drives the prediction

Hiding one configuration choice at a time showed that most choices make no difference at all, and just a couple explain almost everything. Two further findings shaped the recommendation:

  • One configuration type is rare in the history, so predictions for it carry more uncertainty. It deserves an extra buffer, not blind trust.
  • Some variation cannot be explained by configuration at all: cars with identical setups still differ in actual test time, by up to a minute. That points to process or equipment, not to the model.

Keeping the simpler model

A neural network was tried as a challenger. The pass/fail rule was fixed before training: it had to beat the Random Forest by more than a practical-difference threshold of 0.3 seconds. It did not, and the result held across three random seeds.

The Random Forest won at every training-set size tested, its lead widened on categories the model had not seen, and an entity-embedding fix for rare categories gave no accuracy gain and ten times more seed-to-seed variance. Decision: keep the Random Forest.

From the original idea to what was built

The first idea was a scheduling assistant: score every car as its configuration is finalised and sequence the day's queue by expected duration. What the team actually built was a review tool that flags every past car where the predicted time missed the real outcome by more than 5%, so an engineer can inspect and compare similar cases. A time-lapse demo dashboard replays about 800 cars through the bench for presentations.

Next steps recommended to the (hypothetical) stakeholders: pilot on one line alongside current practice, investigate the identical-configuration variance with the process team, and retrain quarterly.

Limits

  • A public Kaggle dataset, not live production data, and an offline evaluation. Nothing was piloted on a real line.
  • No business threshold for “accurate enough to act on” was available, so the recommendation is a pilot, not a rollout.
  • The weakest area is the rare configuration, where the model should not be trusted without a buffer.

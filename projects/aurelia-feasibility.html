Onboarding Assistant: Go or No-Go?

A feasibility study on whether an AI assistant should answer new-hire questions at a fictional software company. The answer: a conditional go, building the low-risk option now and gating the ambitious one.

A fictional case. Aurelia Software GmbH is a made-up company from a course case. The figures below are the case's own estimates, marked as unmeasured in the project's evidence log, and are not real data.

The question

New hires at Aurelia wait one to three days for answers to questions the company already knows how to answer, and an estimated 15 to 20 questions come up per cohort. Should the company build an AI assistant to answer them? The assignment was to decide, and then describe the work for the approved scope, not to build the product.

Three options, compared fairly

OptionWhat it isCharacter
A · Business as usualHandbook, onboarding buddy and an inboxNo change, no new risk, no improvement
B · Knowledge baseAn owned, searchable set of answersLow risk, solid value
C · AI assistantAn assistant that answers questions from company documentsHigher upside, higher exposure, least proven

All three were scored on the same eight dimensions, with every rating carrying its evidence, a confidence level and the next step to validate it. Every claim in the project was labelled before it was trusted: evidence, estimate, assumption, constraint, risk or unknown. About 35 claims were classified, five flagged as critical, and none was upgraded to fact without proof.

Three hard gates

For the AI option, three conditions can stop it outright, whatever else scores well. A gate is not a point deduction; failing one is a no-go for that option.

  1. Hard gateWorks-council clearance
  2. Hard gateEscalation safety test
  3. Hard gateNo sensitive-data leak

The decision: conditional go, in stages

  1. Build Option BThe knowledge base. Approved now: content audit, platform, ownership, launch and adoption report.
  2. Validate Option CA bounded, gated pilot: legal and works-council clearance, prototype and evaluation, escalation safety test.
  3. Decide on building CA separate, future call, only after the gates and conditions are met.

Nine conditions gate any future build of the AI assistant. Three are hard gates; the rest are strong conditions:

  1. GateWorks-council clearance
  2. GateEscalation safety test
  3. Quality threshold met
  4. Owner named
  5. Question volume confirmed
  6. Lawful basis documented
  7. Correction path defined
  8. Effort fits capacity
  9. GateSensitive data excluded

The approved scope was broken down into a work breakdown structure (17 work packages) that follows the 100% rule: all the scope, none extra. Each condition traces to a named work package.

Deliverables

  • A decision memo and a feasibility matrix.
  • An assumptions-and-evidence log with every claim labelled.
  • A work breakdown structure with a dictionary.
  • An executive brief, a process walkthrough and a clearly labelled demo of the assistant.

The process walkthrough also documents how AI was used while producing the analysis: drafting and review passes were accepted, corrected or rejected on the record (22 logged decisions), and the decision itself stayed with the human team.

The demo, and what it is not

The prototype page is a non-functional illustration. Its answers are canned and matched by keyword from a fictional document set. It exists to make two design decisions tangible: every answer cites a source, and questions that touch the safety or legal boundary are escalated to a person instead of answered. It is not evidence that an assistant like this works.

Limits

  • Fictional company, fictional documents, and estimates (waiting times, question volumes) that were never measured.
  • No real users, no works council and no actual legal review were involved. The gates describe what would have to be true, not what was tested.
